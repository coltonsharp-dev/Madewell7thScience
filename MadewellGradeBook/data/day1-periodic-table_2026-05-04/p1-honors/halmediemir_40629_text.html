──── SECTION 1 — THE ORGANIZING PROBLEM ────

Q1 · Before Mendeleev, some scientists organized elements alphabetically. What is the main problem with an alphabetical system?
Answer: A · Alphabetical order tells you nothing about how an element actually behaves or reacts

Q2 · Mendeleev's repeating pattern gave us the word that names the table today — the _____ table.
Answer: C · Periodic — the word for a repeating pattern, naming the table after its key feature

──── SECTION 2 — ATOMIC NUMBER ─────────────

Q3 · The number 11 in sodium's element box tells you that sodium has ___.
Answer: C · An atomic mass of 11 atomic mass units

Q4 · Why is atomic number a BETTER organizing tool than atomic mass?
Answer: C · No two elements can ever share the same atomic number — it uniquely identifies each element

──── SECTION 3 — PERIODS & GROUPS ──────────

Q5 · Sodium (Na) is in Period 3. What does this tell you about its electrons?
Answer: C · Sodium's electrons fill 3 energy shells around the nucleus

Q6 · Li, Na, and K are all in Group 1. What does this predict about their behavior?
Answer: B · They all behave similarly — they are highly reactive metals that form compounds the same way

──── SECTION 4 — THREE REGIONS ─────────────

Q7 · Iron (Fe) is found in the large center-left block of the periodic table. Iron belongs to the ___.
Answer: C · Metals — it is in the large metal region that makes up most of the table

Q8 · Silicon conducts electricity better than a nonmetal but worse than a true metal. Silicon is most likely a ___.
Answer: D · Metalloid — it has properties of both metals and nonmetals, making it ideal for semiconductors

──── ESSENTIAL QUESTION ─────────────────────
Why is the periodic table organized by atomic number rather than any other property?
(Using at least TWO examples, explain: (1) what atomic number tells us, (2) why this makes the table useful as a scientific tool)

Response:
This arrangement is crucial for science because the atomic number acts as a unique identifier for each element; specifically defining its chemical behavior and electron configuration—which dictates its position in the table. For example, Carbon (atomic number 6) has six protons and forms the foundation of organic chemistry, while Oxygen (atomic number 8) has eight protons and is highly electronegative. By ordering elements based on this fundamental proton count, the table becomes a powerful, useful tool that allows chemists to predict element reactivity, locate elements with similar properties in the same column, and even predict the properties of elements that haven't been discovered yet.
