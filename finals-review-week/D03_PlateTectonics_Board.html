01 · Engage

EARTH'S
FOUR LAYERS

1
Crust — thinnest layer (5–70 km). Solid rock. Two kinds: oceanic (basalt, denser) and continental (granite, lighter).
2
Mantle — thickest layer (~2,900 km). Mostly solid rock, but it can flow very slowly because of intense heat — like Silly Putty.
3
Outer Core — liquid iron and nickel (~2,200 km thick). Its motion generates Earth's magnetic field.
4
Inner Core — solid iron and nickel (~1,200 km radius). Hotter than the outer core, but stays solid because of crushing pressure.
Two related terms
Lithosphere = crust + the top, brittle part of the mantle. The plates ARE pieces of the lithosphere. Asthenosphere = the warm, partially soft mantle just below; the plates float and slide on it.
02 · History

WEGENER &
PANGAEA

Alfred Wegener (1912) noticed Africa and South America fit together like puzzle pieces. He proposed a single supercontinent — Pangaea — that had broken apart. His idea was rejected for ~50 years because he couldn't explain HOW continents could move. The mechanism was finally explained by plate tectonics in the 1960s.
Evidence 1 · Coastlines
Africa and South America's coasts fit together like puzzle pieces. Especially clear if you match the continental shelves rather than the shorelines.
Evidence 2 · Fossils
Identical fossils (like Mesosaurus and Glossopteris) appear on continents now separated by oceans — too far for these species to have crossed.
Evidence 3 · Climate & Rocks
Glacial scratches in Africa, India, South America & Australia all point one direction → they were once at the South Pole together. Matching mountain ranges (Appalachians ↔ Caledonian).
After Pangaea
Pangaea split into Laurasia (the north — became N. America, Europe, most of Asia) and Gondwana (the south — became S. America, Africa, Antarctica, India, Australia). Both kept breaking up.
03 · The Engine

WHAT MAKES
PLATES MOVE

Hot rock in the mantle rises (hot stuff rises). Near the cooler crust it cools, becomes denser, and sinks back down. That round-trip is a convection current. Many of these currents act like conveyor belts dragging the lithospheric plates above them.
Why hot rock rises: heat makes molecules move faster and spread out, lowering density. Less dense things rise. Once near the cooler surface they shed heat, get denser, sink. The cycle never stops because Earth's interior is still cooling from its formation 4.5 billion years ago.
04 · Boundaries Part 1

DIVERGENT
→ MOVE APART

What happens
Plates move apart. Magma from the mantle rises into the gap, cools, and forms new crust. The Earth gets wider here.
Features & examples
Underwater: mid-ocean ridges (like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — the longest mountain range on Earth). On land: rift valleys (like the East African Rift — Africa is splitting in two).
05 · Boundaries Part 2

CONVERGENT
→ COLLIDE

Continent ↔ Continent
Both are too thick/light to subduct. Crust crumples upward into mountains.

Example: Himalayas — India ramming into Eurasia. Still rising.
Ocean ↔ Continent
The denser oceanic plate subducts (sinks under). Forms a deep ocean trench + a volcanic mountain range on land.

Example: Andes Mountains — Nazca plate subducting under South America.
Ocean ↔ Ocean
Older / denser plate subducts. Forms a deep trench + a curved chain of volcanic islands.

Example: Japan, Philippines, Aleutian Islands.
Why subduction makes volcanoes
As the subducting plate sinks, water trapped in its rocks is squeezed out. That water lowers the melting point of the surrounding mantle, producing magma — which rises and forms volcanoes above.
06 · Boundaries Part 3

TRANSFORM
→ SLIDE PAST

What happens
Plates slide horizontally past each other in opposite directions. No new crust is created, none is destroyed. They get stuck on each other, build up pressure, and snap free — that snap is an earthquake.
Famous example
San Andreas Fault in California. The Pacific Plate slides north past the North American Plate at about 5 cm/year. Cause of the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and many others. No volcanoes here — only quakes.

08 · Side Story

HOT SPOTS &
RING OF FIRE

Hot Spots
A mantle plume — a column of unusually hot, rising rock — burns through the middle of a tectonic plate. As the plate slowly slides over it, a chain of volcanoes forms.

Hawaii: the Big Island is currently above the hot spot. Older Hawaiian islands to the northwest were over the same hot spot millions of years ago.
Ring of Fire
A horseshoe-shaped zone around the edges of the Pacific Ocean where ~75% of the world's volcanoes and ~90% of its earthquakes happen. It traces the convergent boundaries between the Pacific Plate and surrounding plates (Japan, Philippines, west coast of the Americas, etc.).
