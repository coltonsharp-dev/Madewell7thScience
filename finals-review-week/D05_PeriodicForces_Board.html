01 · Engage

INSIDE
THE ATOM

1
Proton · positive charge (+1) · in the nucleus · its count = atomic number · defines what element it is.
2
Neutron · no charge (0) · also in the nucleus · proton count + neutron count = mass number.
3
Electron · negative charge (−1) · in shells (orbits) around the nucleus · in a neutral atom, electron count = proton count.
4
Nucleus · the dense center of the atom · contains protons + neutrons · is where ~99.9% of the atom's mass is.
Worked example · Carbon-12
Atomic # = 6 → 6 protons. Mass # = 12 → 12 − 6 = 6 neutrons. Neutral atom → 6 electrons.
02 · Reading the Table

PERIODS,
GROUPS, FAMILIES

Periods · Rows (1–7)
Tell you how many electron shells the atom has. Hydrogen (Period 1) has 1 shell. Sodium (Period 3) has 3 shells. Going down a group = adding shells = bigger atom.
Groups · Columns (1–18)
Tell you how many valence electrons (outer-shell electrons) the atom has. Same group = same valence count = similar chemical behavior. That's why Group 1 elements all react like sodium does.
Memory tricks
PERIOD = ROW (think of a sentence ending — period at the end of a horizontal line). GROUP = COLUMN (think of a "group" lining up in a single file vertical column).
famous families Group 1 · Alkali Metals Group 2 · Alkaline Earth Group 17 · Halogens Group 18 · Noble Gases
03 · Categories

METALS, NONMETALS,
METALLOIDS

Metals · ~75% of all elements
Left of staircase line.
Shiny, conductive, malleable (bend), ductile (stretch into wire), usually solid at room temp (mercury is the exception).

Examples: Fe, Cu, Au, Na, Mg.
Nonmetals · ~17 elements
Right of staircase line + hydrogen.
Dull, brittle if solid, poor conductors. Often gases at room temp.

Examples: O, N, C, Cl, S, plus all noble gases (He, Ne, Ar...).
Metalloids · The Staircase
On the staircase line.
Half metal, half nonmetal. Often semiconductors — useful for electronics.

Examples: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
Why silicon matters
Silicon (Si) is the most famous metalloid — every computer chip, phone, and solar panel runs on its semiconductor properties. The "Silicon Valley" is named after this element.
04 · The Three Non-Contact Forces

FORCES THAT
REACH ACROSS SPACE

Gravity
Always attractive. Acts between any two objects with mass.

Strength depends on: mass (more mass = stronger pull) and distance (further = weaker, by a lot).

Examples: what holds you to Earth, the Moon to Earth, planets to the Sun.
Electric Force
Attract OR repel. Opposite charges (+ / −) attract; like charges (+/+ or −/−) repel.

Strength depends on: charge magnitude and distance.

Examples: static cling, lightning, balloon on hair.
Magnetic Force
Attract OR repel. Opposite poles (N–S) attract; like poles (N–N or S–S) repel.

Strength depends on: magnet strength and distance.

Examples: fridge magnets, Earth's magnetic field, MRI machines, electric motors.
05 · How Static Works

STATIC
ELECTRICITY

"Static" just means charge that isn't flowing. When two materials rub together, electrons can hop from one to the other — leaving one positive (lost electrons) and one negative (gained electrons).
Why does the balloon stick to the wall?
The negative balloon pushes electrons away from the surface of the wall, leaving the wall's surface positive. Opposite charges attract → balloon sticks. Same physics is behind lightning — clouds build up huge separations of charge, then a giant spark equalizes everything.
06 · Distance Rules

FORCES GET
WEAKER FAST

All three non-contact forces share an important rule: they get weaker as objects get farther apart. They don't have a sharp "stop" distance — they keep going forever — but they fade quickly.
Real example
A magnet on your fridge: pull it 1 cm away — barely holds. 5 cm away — no effect. The force shrinks much faster than you'd expect. Same with gravity: that's why you don't feel a pull from Mars even though Mars has gravity (Mars is just very far).
07 · Big Connection

ATOMS &
FORCES

Both topics this week are actually connected. The reason atoms behave the way they do — bonding, sharing electrons, forming everything from water to your DNA — is because of the electric force between the protons in the nucleus and the electrons around them.
Static cling
Electrons jump between materials → opposite charges attract.
Lightning
Massive electron buildup → gigantic spark balances the charge.
Why solids feel solid
Negative electron clouds of atoms repel each other → you can't push your hand through wood.
