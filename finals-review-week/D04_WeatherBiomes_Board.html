Memory line
"Climate is what you expect. Weather is what you get." A snowy day in Phoenix in February is weather (unusual). A summer that averages 95°F+ for 30 years is climate.
02 · Why Climates Differ

LATITUDE
CONTROLS CLIMATE

Sunlight near the equator hits Earth almost straight on, so its energy is concentrated. Sunlight at the poles hits at a shallow angle, spreading the same energy over a much larger area — much weaker per square meter. That's why the equator is hot and the poles are cold, every season.
Knock-on effect
Latitude → temperature → evaporation → cloud formation → precipitation → biome distribution. So tropical regions are warm AND wet (rainforests). Polar regions are cold AND dry (tundra).
03 · Extreme Weather

STORMS THAT
MAKE THE NEWS

Hurricane
How it forms: over warm ocean water (≥80 °F) near the equator. Warm water evaporates → rising warm humid air → spinning storm.

Hazards: 75+ mph winds, storm surge (wall of seawater), flooding rain.

Weakens over: land or cold water (loses fuel).
Tornado
How it forms: during severe thunderstorms when warm and cold air masses collide and rotate, dropping a funnel from the cloud to the ground.

Hazards: winds 100–300+ mph, narrow path of total destruction.

Where: "Tornado Alley" — central US plains.
Blizzard
How it forms: heavy snow + strong winds (35+ mph) + low visibility for 3+ hours.

Hazards: hypothermia, frostbite, whiteout driving conditions, power outages, snow load on roofs.

Where: mid-to-high latitudes in winter.
Drought
Long stretch of below-average precipitation — months to years. Crops fail, reservoirs drop, wildfire risk climbs. Especially relevant here in the Southwest. Phoenix has been in a 20+ year megadrought.
04 · The Water Cycle

WATER ON
REPEAT

The total amount of water on Earth never changes — it just keeps cycling between oceans, atmosphere, land, and living things.
Evaporation
Liquid water → water vapor (gas). Sun heats it. Happens mostly from oceans.
Condensation
Water vapor → tiny liquid droplets. That's how clouds form.
Precipitation
Drops/crystals fall to Earth as rain, snow, sleet, or hail.
05 · Land Biomes Part 1

HOT &
WET TO DRY

Tropical Rainforest
Warm year-round (~80°F). 80+ inches of rain a year. Layers: emergent, canopy, understory, forest floor. Highest biodiversity on Earth.

Examples: Amazon (S. America), Congo (Africa), SE Asia.
Savanna
Tropical grassland. Warm year-round. Distinct wet and dry seasons. Grasses, scattered trees (acacia).

Examples: African plains (lions, giraffes, zebras).
Desert
< 10 inches of rain a year. Hot or cold (yes, cold deserts exist!). Cacti, succulents, lizards, kangaroo rats.

Example: Sonoran (right outside!).
memory tip Rainforest = wet + warm Savanna = warm + seasonal rain Desert = warm/cold + dry
06 · Land Biomes Part 2

SEASONAL
TO ARCTIC

Temperate Forest
Four distinct seasons. Cold winters, warm summers. Deciduous trees (oak, maple) drop leaves each fall.

Examples: eastern US, Europe, parts of Asia.
Taiga (Boreal Forest)
Largest land biome. Cold, long winters. Coniferous trees (spruce, pine, fir) — needles instead of leaves to handle the cold.

Example: Canada, Russia, Scandinavia.
Tundra
Permafrost (permanently frozen ground). Very cold, very dry, short summer. No trees — just lichens, mosses, low shrubs.

Example: Arctic Circle.
07 · Adaptations

HOW LIFE
FITS IN

Organisms have adaptations — body parts or behaviors — that match the abiotic conditions of their biome. Same problem (e.g. low water) gets solved different ways by different species.

08 · Aquatic Biomes

NOT JUST
DRY LAND

Marine Biome (saltwater)
Largest biome on Earth — covers ~70% of the surface. Includes oceans, coral reefs, estuaries.

Coral reefs are the "rainforests of the sea" — incredibly biodiverse, sensitive to warming/acidification.
Freshwater Biome
Lakes, rivers, ponds, wetlands. Less than 3% of Earth's water but supports a huge fraction of biodiversity.

Provides drinking water and irrigation for almost all human civilization.
