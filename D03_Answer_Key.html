Section 1 — Why Atoms Bond · Q1–Q2

MC ANSWER
KEY

Full explanations + distractor notes
Q1
Why do most atoms form chemical bonds with other atoms?
A
Atoms bond because they are attracted to atoms with a higher atomic number
Incorrect. Atomic number determines identity, not bonding attraction. An atom bonds based on electron configuration, not comparative atomic numbers.
B
Atoms bond to reach a full outer electron shell, which is a more stable configuration
Correct answer
C
Atoms bond because bonded atoms are always larger than individual atoms
Incorrect. Atomic size is not the driver of bonding. This conflates atomic size trends with bonding motivation.
D
Atoms bond only when they are in the same period on the periodic table
Incorrect. NaCl (Na = Period 3, Cl = Period 3) is same period — but H₂O (H = Period 1, O = Period 2) also bonds. Period has nothing to do with bonding eligibility.
Q2
The "octet rule" states that atoms tend to form bonds until they have ___.
A
8 protons in their nucleus
Incorrect. Proton count = atomic number and never changes. Oxygen has 8 protons and always will — that has nothing to do with bonding.
B
8 total electron shells around the nucleus
Incorrect. Most atoms have only 1–7 shells. The record is 7 (Period 7 elements). Confuses shell count with electron count.
C
8 electrons in their outermost shell
Correct answer
D
8 bonds with neighboring atoms
Incorrect. The number 8 refers to electrons, not bonds. Carbon (which can form 4 bonds) follows the octet rule perfectly — it needs 4 more electrons to reach 8, so it forms 4 bonds.
Section 2 — Ionic Bonding · Q3–Q4
Q3
In ionic bonding, what happens to the metal atom's valence electron(s)?
A
The metal atom shares its valence electrons equally with the nonmetal
Incorrect. Sharing = covalent bonding. Ionic bonding involves transfer, not sharing. If students choose this, revisit the ionic vs. covalent distinction directly.
B
The metal atom's valence electrons are destroyed during the reaction
Incorrect. Electrons are never created or destroyed in chemical reactions — conservation of matter applies. This reveals a fundamental misconception about what bonds are.
C
The metal atom transfers its valence electrons to the nonmetal atom
Correct answer
D
The metal atom gains additional valence electrons from the nonmetal
Incorrect — this reverses the direction. Metals GIVE electrons (they become positive). Nonmetals GAIN electrons (they become negative). A student choosing D likely has the direction of electron movement backwards.
Q4
After sodium (Na) gives its 1 valence electron to chlorine (Cl) during ionic bonding, sodium becomes ___.
A
Na⁻ — a negatively charged ion with one extra electron
Incorrect — sign is reversed. Na loses an electron, so it has fewer electrons than protons → positive charge. Only if Na gained an electron would it be negative. If a student chooses A, they have the charge sign convention backwards.
B
Na⁺ — a positively charged ion with one fewer electron than protons
Correct answer
C
A neutral atom with a full outer shell and no charge
Incorrect. Na does reach a full outer shell after losing its electron — BUT it is no longer neutral because it now has 11 protons and only 10 electrons. The charge imbalance is the key concept here.
D
A different element entirely, since it lost an electron
Incorrect. Element identity is defined by proton count (atomic number), not electron count. Na still has 11 protons after losing an electron — it is still sodium, just a sodium ION (Na⁺).
Section 3 — Covalent Bonding · Q5–Q6
Q5
Water (H₂O) is formed from two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom — all nonmetals. What type of bond holds water together?
A
Ionic — because oxygen is larger than hydrogen and takes the electrons
Incorrect. Size difference does not determine bond type. The key rule: nonmetal + nonmetal = covalent. Even though oxygen does attract electrons more strongly (electronegativity), water is still classified as a polar covalent bond — sharing, not transfer.
B
Covalent — nonmetal + nonmetal share electrons rather than transfer them
Correct answer
C
Ionic — because water conducts electricity when dissolved
Incorrect. Pure water actually conducts electricity poorly. Dissolved salts in tap water conduct, not the water molecules themselves. Water's ability to dissolve ionic compounds does not make water itself ionic.
D
Neither — water is a special compound that does not follow bonding rules
Incorrect. Water follows covalent bonding rules exactly. It is an excellent teaching example because students are familiar with it — it is predictable, not an exception.
Q6
Which of the following is the BEST way to tell an ionic compound apart from a covalent compound at room temperature?
A
Ionic compounds tend to be solid crystals or salts; covalent compounds are often liquids or gases
Correct answer
B
Ionic compounds are always colorless; covalent compounds always have color
Incorrect. Many ionic compounds are deeply colored — CuSO₄ (blue), KMnO₄ (purple), FeCl₃ (orange-brown). Many covalent compounds are colorless. Color is not a reliable indicator of bond type.
C
Covalent compounds are always heavier than ionic compounds
Incorrect. Mass depends on the specific atoms involved, not the bond type. NaCl (ionic, 58 g/mol) is lighter than many large covalent molecules like fats or proteins.
D
Ionic compounds smell strongly; covalent compounds have no odor
Incorrect. Most ionic compounds (table salt, calcium oxide) have no smell at all. Many covalent compounds — acetone, vinegar, ammonia — have strong odors. This is the reverse of reality.
Section 4 — Ion Charges & Formulas · Q7–Q8
Q7
Magnesium (Mg) is in Group 2. What ion does it form when it reacts with a nonmetal?
A
Mg⁻ — it gains 2 electrons to fill its outer shell
Incorrect. Metals LOSE electrons, not gain them. Mg has only 2 valence electrons — it is far easier to give 2 away than gain 6 more. The charge is also wrong: losing electrons → positive charge.
B
Mg²⁺ — it loses 2 valence electrons, leaving it with a 2+ charge
Correct answer
C
Mg¹⁺ — it loses 1 electron because that is easier than losing 2
Incorrect. Mg has 2 valence electrons and both must be removed to reach a full outer shell (the noble gas configuration of neon). Losing only 1 leaves Mg with an odd electron in its outer shell — still unstable.
D
Mg⁰ — metals do not form ions
Incorrect. Forming ions is literally what metals do in ionic bonding. This is the core of the lesson. Mg²⁺ is one of the most common ions in biology — it is found in bones, enzymes, and chlorophyll.
Q8
Calcium (Ca, Group 2) forms Ca²⁺ ions. Oxygen (O, Group 16) forms O²⁻ ions. What is the correct formula for the ionic compound they form?
A
Ca₂O — two calcium atoms are needed to balance one oxygen
Incorrect. This would give total charge of 2(2+) + 1(2−) = 4+ − 2 = 2+ total. Not balanced. The student has the logic of which ion needs two backwards — Ca already has 2+ and O already has 2−, so they balance 1:1.
B
CaO — one Ca²⁺ balances one O²⁻, charges cancel to zero
Correct answer — Ca²⁺ (2+) + O²⁻ (2−) = 0. Balanced.
C
CaO₂ — oxygen always appears twice in ionic compounds
Incorrect. There is no rule that oxygen appears twice. CaO₂ would require a 4− charge on the oxygen side (2 × 2−) against Ca's 2+ — unbalanced. O₂²⁻ (peroxide ion) exists but is a different species.
D
Ca₂O₂ — both subscripts must always be written even if equal
Incorrect. Ca₂O₂ simplifies to CaO (we always reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio). The student may be confusing compound formulas with molecular formulas. In ionic compounds, we always reduce.

Essential Question · 4 points

ESSENTIAL
QUESTION

The Question
Using what you know about valence electrons, explain why sodium (Na) and chlorine (Cl) react to form NaCl — and why that compound is stable when neither sodium nor chlorine alone is stable. Include how many valence electrons each starts with and what happens during the bond.
4
Full Credit
Correctly states Na has 1 valence electron and Cl has 7 valence electrons. Accurately describes electron transfer (Na gives 1 electron to Cl). Identifies the resulting ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) by name or charge. Explains why NaCl is stable — both ions now have full outer shells (8 electrons). Uses at least 3 complete sentences. Specific element names used throughout.
3
Strong Partial Credit
Correct valence electron counts AND accurate description of the bond process, BUT missing explanation of WHY NaCl is stable (doesn't connect to full outer shells). OR: Correct valence counts + stability explanation, but bond process is vague (says "they combine" without describing the electron transfer).
2
Partial Credit
Correct definition of ionic bonding in general terms (metal gives electron to nonmetal) but does NOT apply it specifically to Na and Cl. OR: Correctly identifies valence counts but confuses the direction of electron movement (says Cl gives to Na, or says they share). OR: Response is only 1–2 sentences with incomplete reasoning.
1
Minimal Credit
Attempted response using some relevant vocabulary (ions, bonding, electrons) but reasoning is incorrect, circular, or does not connect to valence electrons. Example: "Na and Cl bond because they are opposite elements and need each other." No specific electron counts or bond mechanics.
0
No Credit
No response, completely off-topic, or response only restates the question without any explanation.

Target Response — 4-Point Example

What a full-credit response sounds like
Sodium (Na) has 1 valence electron and chlorine (Cl) has 7 valence electrons. During ionic bonding, sodium gives its 1 outer electron to chlorine. This makes sodium a positively charged ion (Na⁺) because it now has more protons than electrons, and it makes chlorine a negatively charged ion (Cl⁻) because it gained an extra electron. The opposite charges attract and hold the compound NaCl together. NaCl is stable because after the transfer, both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have full outer shells — Na⁺ has the electron configuration of neon and Cl⁻ has the configuration of argon. Neither Na nor Cl alone is stable because Na has 1 unpaired electron it wants to give away, and Cl has 7 outer electrons with 1 empty spot it wants to fill.

Key Phrases to Look For

✓"1 valence electron" / "one outer electron" for Na
✓"7 valence electrons" / "seven outer electrons" for Cl
✓"gives" / "transfers" / "loses" its electron (Na → active giver)
✓"receives" / "gains" / "accepts" (Cl → active receiver)
✓"Na⁺" and "Cl⁻" or "positive ion" and "negative ion" / "cation" and "anion"
✓"full outer shell" / "8 electrons" / "stable configuration" as reason NaCl is stable
✗Penalize if student says they "share" electrons — that describes covalent bonding
✗Penalize if student reverses direction (Cl gives to Na) — electron goes from Na to Cl
Watch For · Common Misconceptions

KEY
MISCONCEPTIONS

Misconception 1 · Sharing vs Transfer
Students frequently say ionic bonds involve "sharing" electrons. Fix: "In ionic bonding, the metal electron is completely transferred — it goes to live with the nonmetal permanently. Sharing is covalent. Think of it like giving away your lunch versus eating it together."
Misconception 2 · Charge Direction
Students reverse the charges — thinking the atom that gives electrons becomes negative. Fix: "If you have 11 protons and give away 1 electron, you now have 11 protons and 10 electrons. More positive charges than negative → positive ion. Giving = losing negative things = becoming more positive."
Misconception 3 · Element identity changes
Students think Na⁺ is a different element. Fix: "Element identity is defined by the number of PROTONS — the atomic number. Na⁺ still has 11 protons. It is still sodium. It is just a sodium ION — a sodium atom with a charge. Compare it to yourself wearing a hat: you are still you, just slightly different."
Misconception 4 · HCl must be ionic (it has H)
Students assume HCl is ionic because it "conducts electricity when dissolved." Fix: "H is a nonmetal. Cl is a nonmetal. Nonmetal + nonmetal = covalent. When HCl dissolves in water, it ionizes — the ions come from the dissolution, not from the original bond type."
Misconception 5 · Subscripts always needed in formulas
Students write Ca₂O₂ instead of CaO. Fix: "We always reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio, just like simplifying fractions. Ca₂O₂ reduces to CaO because the ratio is 1:1. The subscripts only appear when the ratio isn't 1:1 — like Na₂O, where we need 2 Na⁺ to balance one O²⁻."
Lesson Summary · Core Concepts

CORE
CONCEPTS

Day 3 · Chemical Bonding
Why Atoms Bond
Atoms bond to reach a full outer shell (octet rule — 8 valence electrons). Noble gases already have full shells → unreactive. All other elements bond to achieve that stability.
Ionic Bonding
Metal + Nonmetal. Metal transfers valence electrons to nonmetal. Both become ions. Opposite charges attract. Forms crystals/salts. Conducts when dissolved.
Covalent Bonding
Nonmetal + Nonmetal. Electrons are shared between atoms. Forms molecules. Often liquid or gas at room temperature. Does not conduct electricity.
Ion Charges
Group 1 → 1+ · Group 2 → 2+ · Group 16 → 2− · Group 17 → 1− · Formulas must balance to zero total charge.
NaCl Explained
Na (1 VE) gives its electron to Cl (7 VE). Na → Na⁺ (10 e⁻, 11 p⁺). Cl → Cl⁻ (18 e⁻, 17 p⁺). Both now have full outer shells. Opposite charges attract → stable compound.
Connecting to Day 2
Valence electrons (Day 2) are exactly what drives bonding (Day 3). Group number → valence electrons → ion charge → compound formula. The chain is direct.
